## 1. Introduction to Honeypots

A **honeypot** is a fake proxy/computer system intended to attract and trap unauthorized attackers penetrating an organization's network.
* Logs traffic and forwards complaints to ISPs.
* It has **no authorized activity** or production value; any interaction is highly likely malicious.
* **Uses:** Detecting attacks, information gathering, and research (logging port access attempts and monitoring keystrokes for early warnings).

---

## 2. Types of Honeypots (Based on Design)

### Low-interaction Honeypots
Emulate only a limited number of services and applications. If an attacker does something unexpected, it generates an error.
* **Characteristics:** Cannot be completely compromised. Collects high-level information (network probes, worm activities).
* **Examples:**
  * `tiny-ssh-honeypot`
  * **KFSensor:** Implements vulnerable services/Trojans to monitor TCP/UDP/ICMP ports. Raises alerts about port scanning/DoS.
  * **Honeytrap:** Observes attacks against TCP/UDP. Runs as a daemon, concatenates strings, and automatically attempts to download files requested by the attacker. Identifies `HTTP_URIs`.

### Medium-interaction Honeypots
Simulate a real OS as well as applications and services. Provide greater misconception than low-interaction.
* **Characteristics:** Captures more complex data but can only respond to preconfigured commands. Increased risk of intrusion.
* **Examples:**
  * `Honeygrove`
  * `Kippo`
  * **Cowrie:** SSH and Telnet honeypot logging brute-force and shell interactions. Medium interaction mode (shell) emulates a UNIX system via Python. High interaction mode (proxy) functions as a proxy to observe behavior on another system.

### High-interaction Honeypots
Do not emulate; they run actual vulnerable services or software on production systems with real OS.
* **Characteristics:** Can be completely compromised. Captures complete information (attack techniques, tools, intent).
* **Example:** **Honeynet**
  * An entire network of computers designed to be attacked.
  * Activities (encrypted SSH, emails, uploads) are monitored by inserting kernel modules.
  * Controlled by a **honeywall gateway** allowing inbound traffic but controlling outbound traffic to prevent harm to non-honeynet computers.

### Pure Honeypots
Emulate the real production network of a target organization to distract attackers from critical systems and provide early warnings.

## 3. Types of Honeypots (Based on Deployment)

1. **Production Honeypots:** Deployed inside the production network. Generally fall under the low-interaction category. Improves overall security by finding internal flaws and capturing limited adversary data.
2. **Research Honeypots:** High-interaction honeypots deployed by research institutes/military to gain in-depth knowledge of TTPs. Does not contribute to direct company security.

---

## 4. Types of Honeypots (Based on Deception Technology)

* **Malware Honeypots:** Simulated with known vulnerabilities (outdated APIs, vulnerable SMBv1) to emulate Trojans, viruses, and backdoors.
* **Database Honeypots:** Employ fake databases vulnerable to SQL injection/enumeration, tricking attackers into stealing fake credit card/employee data.
* **Spam Honeypots:** Consist of mail servers that deliberately accept emails to target spammers abusing open mail relays and open proxies.
* **Email Honeypots (Email Traps):** Fake email addresses distributed across the internet and dark web to attract malicious emails.
* **Spider Honeypots (Spider Traps):** Emulated fake websites designed to trap web crawlers/spiders attempting to extract URLs, contact details, or directories.

## 6. Detecting Honeypots

Attackers use service fingerprinting, response times, and MAC addresses to avoid detection.

1. **Fingerprinting the Running Service:** Discrepancies between claimed and actual service behavior.
   * *Command:* `nmap -sV -p 80 <target_ip>`
2. **Analyzing Response Time:** Honeypots exhibit slower/inconsistent response times due to extra logging layers.
   * *Command:* `nmap -p 80 --scan-delay 1s --max-retries 5 target_ip`
3. **Analyzing MAC Address:** Identifying virtualization technology (e.g., VMWare) by looking at the specific MAC prefix (OUI).
   * *Command:* `arp-scan --interface=eth0 --localnet`
4. **Enumerating Unexpected Open Ports:** High number of open ports without a clear purpose (e.g., 21, 22 open alongside 80/443 on a standard web server).
   * *Command:* `nmap -p-<target_ip>`
5. **Analyzing System Configuration and Metadata:** Examining SSH welcome messages, HTTP headers, and default/outdated banners.

## 7. Detecting and Defeating Specific Honeypots

* **Layer 7 Tar Pits:** Respond slowly to incoming SMTP commands. Detected by measuring latency.
* **Layer 4 Tar Pits (e.g., Labrea):** Manipulate the TCP/IP stack. Accept the TCP connection and switch to a **zero-window size**, blocking further data transfer. Detected by analyzing TCP window size.
* **Layer 2 Tar Pits:** Block internal threat network penetration. Identified by ARP responses from a unique MAC address (`00:00:0F:FF:FF:FF`) acting as a black hole.
* **VMware-based Honeypots:** Detected by analyzing MAC addresses against IEEE standards assigned to VMWare Inc.
* **Honeyd Honeypot:** Detected by performing **time-based TCP fingerprinting (SYN proxy behavior)**. Honeyd's response to manual SYN requests differs from a normal computer.
* **User-Mode Linux (UML) Honeypot:** Detected by analyzing UML-specific information in `/proc/mounts`, `/proc/interrupts`, and `/proc/cmdline`.
* **Snort_inline Honeypot:** Capturing modified outgoing packets through another host system.
* **Fake AP:** Access points that send 802.11b beacon frames but produce no actual network traffic.
* **Bait and Switch Honeypots:** Actively redirect malicious traffic. Identified by looking at specific TCP/IP parameters (RTT, TTL, TCP timestamp).
